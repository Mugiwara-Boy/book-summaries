Book Notes — 03

Influence

Robert B. Cialdini — The Psychology of Persuasion, New and Expanded Edition (2021; first published 1984)

People rarely weigh every fact before saying yes. They react to a handful of reliable cues, and anyone who controls those cues can make the yes nearly automatic.

01 — The Core Idea

Click, whirr: the yes that runs itself

Cialdini, a social psychologist at Arizona State, wrote the book after admitting he was an easy mark. He was the person who bought what he didn't need from salespeople and gave to causes he hadn't heard of. To understand why, he spent about three years undercover in the training programmes of the people who persuade for a living: car dealers, door-to-door sellers, fundraisers, advertisers and recruiters. He found that the endless variety of tactics drew on only a few psychological principles. The 1984 edition named six. The 2021 edition adds a seventh.

The principles work because of something animal behaviourists call fixed-action patterns. A mother turkey cares for her chicks only when she hears a “cheep-cheep” sound. A deaf turkey ignores, and even attacks, her own young. A stuffed polecat, the turkey's natural enemy, gets mothered as long as it plays a recording of that sound. One feature of the situation triggers the whole behaviour, like pressing play on a tape: click, and the tape runs, whirr.

Humans run tapes too, and for good reason. Life is too fast and too complex to analyse every decision, so we lean on shortcuts like “expensive means good”, “experts are usually right” and “if everyone's doing it, it's probably fine”. They are right most of the time, which is exactly what makes them exploitable. Cialdini's favourite example is a jewellery shop owner in Arizona who couldn't move a case of turquoise. Leaving on a trip, she scribbled a note telling an employee to sell everything in the case at half price. The employee read the “½” as “2”, doubled the prices, and the stock sold out. Tourists who couldn't judge turquoise used price as a stand-in for quality.

02 — The Mechanism

Two quiet amplifiers

Before the seven principles, the book shows two effects that sit underneath many of them.

The power of “because” (Ellen Langer's copier study): people asking to cut a queue at a copier were let in about 60% of the time with no reason. With a real reason (“because I'm in a rush”) it was about 94%. With an empty one (“because I have to make some copies”) it was still about 93%. For small requests, the word “because” triggers compliance almost regardless of what follows it.

The contrast principle: we judge things relative to what we saw just before. A clothing salesman sells the expensive suit first, so the sweater looks cheap. An estate agent shows two run-down “setup” houses before the one they want to sell. Nothing about the product changes, only the reference point. The trick leaves no trace, because the buyer believes they reached the judgement on their own.

Together these explain why the seven levers below are so hard to resist. Each one triggers a shortcut that is usually correct, inside a frame the persuader controls. You can't stop every one by being smarter. You defend yourself by noticing when a shortcut is being faked.

03 — The Seven Levers

Seven shortcuts, each with a defense

Each lever is described first as a force, then with the move that disarms it when someone fakes it.

LEVER 01

Reciprocation

Every human society teaches the same rule: if someone does something for you, you owe them. Cialdini calls it one of the glues of civilisation, because it let people give first without fear of losing out. It is also easy to exploit. In Dennis Regan's 1971 study, a stranger who handed subjects an unasked-for Coke later sold them about twice as many raffle tickets as one who gave nothing, and how much they liked him barely mattered. The debt drove it. The Hare Krishna airport “gift” of a flower worked the same way.

Two details make it sharper. The gift works best when it is meaningful, unexpected and personal. In a restaurant study, one mint with the bill raised tips about 3%, two mints about 14%, and one mint followed by the waiter turning back to offer a second “just for you” raised them about 23%. Second, a concession counts as a gift. In “rejection-then-retreat”, you make a large request, accept the no, then retreat to the smaller one you wanted. In Cialdini's zoo study, asking students to chaperone delinquent kids for a day got 17% agreement cold. After first asking them to volunteer two years as counsellors, it got 50%. The retreat felt like a favour, and the smaller ask looked small by contrast.

How to say noAccept real favours gratefully. When a “gift” turns out to be a sales device, re-label it in your head as a tactic. The rule obliges you to return favours, not tactics.
LEVER 02

Liking

We say yes to people we like, and liking is easy to manufacture. Joe Girard, the Guinness record holder for car sales, mailed every past customer a card each month that said little more than that he liked them. Tupperware parties sell through the hostess's friendship, not the product.

The book lists what drives liking: physical attractiveness (a halo that makes people seem smarter and kinder), similarity (in background, interests and even dress), compliments (which work even when we suspect them), contact and cooperation toward a shared goal (the jigsaw classroom ended hostilities that mere contact had made worse), and association with good things, which is why messengers get blamed for bad news and brands rent celebrities.

How to say noDon't try to spot each tactic. Notice when you've come to like someone unusually fast, then mentally separate the person from the deal and judge the offer alone.
LEVER 03

Social Proof

When we don't know what is right, we look at what people like us are doing. That's why canned laughter works even though everyone says they hate it. Social proof is most powerful under two conditions: uncertainty, when the situation is new or ambiguous, and similarity, when the crowd looks like us. Bystanders fail to help in emergencies partly because each person reads the others' calm as proof that nothing is wrong. The cure is to single out one person and give them a specific task.

It can also backfire. At Arizona's Petrified Forest, a sign deploring how many visitors stole wood increased theft, because it told readers stealing was normal. In hotel studies by Cialdini's team, telling guests that most previous guests in that same room reused their towels beat the standard environmental appeal.

How to say noWatch for faked evidence (staged testimonials, inflated counts). Remember that a crowd can be copying itself, so check the facts at least once.
LEVER 04

Authority

Milgram's obedience studies are the book's darkest proof: about two-thirds of ordinary people kept delivering what they believed were dangerous shocks because a man in a lab coat told them to. Deference to authority is usually sensible. But people respond to the symbols of authority (titles, uniforms, expensive trappings) almost as strongly as to the substance. In one hospital study, nearly every nurse called by an unknown “doctor” started to give a clearly unsafe dose of an unapproved drug.

The subtle point for anyone selling is about trustworthiness. An expert who first admits a small weakness in their own case is believed more on everything that follows, because arguing against your own interest signals honesty.

How to say noAsk two questions. Is this person truly an expert on this specific thing? And how truthful can I expect them to be, given what they gain if I agree?
LEVER 05

Scarcity

Things look more valuable when they are less available, and the thought of losing something moves us more than the thought of gaining the same thing. In Stephen Worchel's cookie study, identical cookies were rated more desirable from a jar holding two than from a jar holding ten. They were rated higher still when the jar went from ten to two because other people had wanted them. Scarcity caused by competition is the strongest kind. Information works the same way. In one beef importer's experiment, buyers told about a coming shortage, and told that the news was exclusive, ordered about six times as much as buyers told about the shortage alone.

Scarcity also explains reactance: when a freedom is taken away, we want it more. Banned books sell. Forbidden love intensifies, which Cialdini calls the Romeo and Juliet effect.

How to say noThe tell is a rush of urgency that makes it hard to think. Use it as a cue to stop. Then ask why you want the thing. The scarce cookies did not taste any better. If you want to use something rather than just own it, scarcity tells you nothing about its value.
LEVER 06

Commitment & Consistency

Once we take a stand, we feel pressure to behave in line with it, because consistency is admired and saves thinking. Small commitments therefore open the door to big ones. In Freedman and Fraser's study, residents who had agreed to display a tiny “drive safely” sign later agreed to a huge, ugly billboard on their lawn about 76% of the time, against about 17% for people asked cold. The first yes changed how they saw themselves.

The commitments that stick hardest are active (written beats spoken), public, effortful and owned, meaning made without a large outside reward, so people credit themselves. This is how Chinese captors in the Korean War got prisoners to write small pro-communist statements, and why sales teams get customers to fill in the order form themselves. The dark version is the lowball. A car buyer commits at a great price, builds new reasons to want the car, and keeps those reasons after the price “error” is fixed.

How to say noTwo signals help. The stomach signal is feeling trapped into something you know is wrong. The heart-of-hearts signal comes from asking: knowing what I know now, would I make the same choice again? If the answer is no, the earlier commitment owes you nothing.
LEVER 07

Unity

The principle added in 2021 is about shared identity, not similarity. The question is not “are you like me?” but “are you one of us?” Unity comes from being together (family, place, tribe) and from acting together (moving in sync, suffering together, building something together). People favour, trust and help those inside their “we” far more than polite similarity would predict.

Its most usable form is co-creation. When people are asked for advice on a plan, rather than their opinion or expectations, they feel more merged with it and become more supportive. Advice puts them on the same side of the table.

How to say noAsk whether the “we” is real and lasting, or a costume put on for this one request.
04 — Synthesis

Detective, not smuggler

The levers stack. A good pitch rarely uses only one. A free sample (reciprocation) given by a likeable rep (liking) with “our most popular” on the label (social proof) and “only while stocks last” (scarcity) is four levers pulling the same way. Cialdini's ethical line is not about whether to use them. It is about whether they are truly present. A detective of influence finds the real social proof, the real expertise and the real scarcity in an offer and points to them. A smuggler fabricates them. Smuggling can win the sale, but it damages the shortcuts everyone relies on. Cialdini argues people should push back hard on fakers, because a society that can't trust its shortcuts has to think through every decision from scratch.

  • Levers you earn over time: social proof, authority and unity can't be faked for long. They come from real results, real expertise and a shared identity, and they compound.
  • Levers you choose in the moment: reciprocation, contrast, “because”, small commitments and honest scarcity are framing decisions you make in every email and meeting.
  • The defense is one habit: when you feel an unusually quick yes, ask which shortcut just fired, and whether the cue was real.

For a founder selling a new category, the biggest point is that prospects are uncertain, and uncertainty is when social proof from similar buyers is strongest. One named result from an exhibitor in the same industry will do more than any feature list. The cheapest lever is reciprocation done properly, meaning a follow-up that gives something useful before it asks for anything. The most underused is unity, because asking a prospect, advisor or investor for advice turns them from judges of your plan into co-authors of it.

06 — Do This

Ten actions, not ten insights

The charts show the shape of the argument. These are the changes to make in your pipeline this week.

0 / 10 done
01Give first in every follow-up. Before any ask, send one thing the prospect can use for their next show, tied to what came up in the conversation. Make it specific to them, unexpected and useful.
02Put a real “because” in every ask. Every demo request, intro request and investor ask carries one honest reason they would care. Cut any ask that has no reason.
03Ask big, then retreat, on the next pilot conversation. Present the full multi-show rollout first, then step back to a single-show pilot as the concession.
04Get a small yes at the booth. Before asking for a meeting, ask for something tiny, like a two-minute look at one past conversation summary. The small yes sets up the bigger one.
05Build one proof point per vertical. One named exhibitor result for each industry you sell into, so every prospect sees someone like them. Show only real results, and drop any proof point that can't be named.
06Name one limit before the strengths in every demo. Say early and plainly what Pravah doesn't do yet. It costs little and makes every claim after it more believable.
07Frame urgency as loss, and only when it's true. Talk about the leads that cool in the days after a show, not features to gain. Never invent deadlines or slots.
08Get the next step written in their words. At the end of every demo, ask the prospect to put the next step and date in the reply email themselves. A written, owned commitment gets kept.
09Ask for advice, not opinion. With advisors, investors and design partners, ask what they would advise on a specific decision. It turns them into co-owners of the plan.
10Run the heart-of-hearts check on your own deals this month. Pick one vendor contract, hire or partnership you committed to early. Ask whether you would choose it again knowing what you know now, and act on the answer.
